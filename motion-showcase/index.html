The Hours of Prayer

The Church’s day of prayer, shown live for Mankato, Minnesota. The hand sweeps the day and lights each hour in turn. Tap an hour to read its prayers, including Prime, the hour left out of the modern breviary.

Times are approximate and calculated for Mankato from today’s sunrise and sunset. The day hours were traditionally counted from sunrise: Terce, Sext, and None fall three, six, and nine hours after it, and Vespers falls at sunset. Matins is shown at 2:00 a.m. and Compline at 9:00 p.m. as clock times.

The workbench: MCU

The whole collection wired to one microcontroller. The MCU reads Minnesota time, turns the day number into an address, and drives twelve modules in order: time, rhythm, prayer, story, teachers, virtue, formation, and last, an act of mercy.

Same day, same outputs: each module picks its entry by day of the year, so the board is repeatable like an address. Solarium zones and lessons come from your Solarium Rhythm file, and the fourteen acts of mercy come from your Mercy Atlas file. The rest are the modules on this page.

Three kinds of traveler

Each traveler moves through a journey differently, so each needs a different kind of support. The Triathlete is the slowest by far, because the race is longer and changes discipline twice.

Sprinter

Who we serve: people with an urgent, short, well-defined move who need an answer fast.

Best staffing: a quick-response desk with clear checklists, on-call coordinators, and fast handoff to a decision.

Marathon Runner

Who we serve: people on a long, steady, predictable path who value consistency over speed.

Best staffing: one dedicated point of contact who stays with them, plus regular check-ins on a set rhythm.

Triathlete

Who we serve: people whose journey is long and changes terrain: pace shifts and so do the skills needed.

Best staffing: a lead coordinator who owns the whole journey, with specialists handing off at each stage.

One circle, then many

One circle at the center, then ring after ring: Adam at the core, Jesus near the rim, and the popes closing the circle. Dots are the 353 entries and channels are the relationships between them, each marked by how well it is supported.

Tap a dot, or slide through the entries

Line styles show support: solid is directly attested, long dashes are supported but qualified, dots are interpretive, and short dashes in red are disputed. The edges are the ones I could source to Scripture, the Vatican list, or named ancient sources. Most entries have no relationship entered yet, so absence of a line is not evidence that none exists.
